Transitional Safeguarding Toolkit

For Adult Social Care practitioners supporting young adults aged 18–21 (up to 25) who have been in care, on a child protection plan or known as a Child in Need.

DRAFT · confirm contacts before use

Start here

This toolkit helps you spot young adults in the transition gap, use the legal powers you already have, and connect them to support that has evidence of working, before they reach a high-risk panel.

Immediate danger? Call 999. Adult safeguarding concern: 01202 123654 (office hours) or 0300 123 9895 (out of hours). Mental health crisis: Connection 0800 652 0190 (24/7).

Who is this for?

Young adults aged 18–21 (up to 25 for care leavers) with a history of care, child protection or Child in Need involvement, especially where there is exploitation, homelessness, poor mental health, substance use or contact with the police.

Why now?

BCP's Preparing for Adulthood Strategy 2025–2027 already commits the council to “a clear pathway for transitional safeguarding”. Ofsted (Dec 2024) found support for care leavers drops off as they approach 21. New Care Leaver Local Offer guidance takes effect on 30 Sept 2026 and expects every council department, ASC included, to help prevent cliff edges at 18.

The key point

You do not need a high-risk panel to act. The Care Act s42 duty covers adults with needs for care and support, whether or not the council is meeting them. A s9 assessment is triggered by the appearance of need.

Young person still in care? Where appropriate, discuss and use this toolkit at their final child in care review before 18, chaired by their Independent Reviewing Officer (IRO). Ask the social worker to invite the named ASC worker.

Six principles of Transitional Safeguarding

1 · Evidence-informed

Research, practice wisdom and lived experience.

2 · Contextual

Peers, places, online spaces, housing and work, not just the individual.

3 · Developmental

Maturity and trauma matter more than age. Brain development continues into the mid-20s.

4 · Relational

A consistent, trusted relationship is the main way support works.

5 · Equalities-orientated

Watch for adultification bias and compounding discrimination.

6 · Participative

Voice and choice, balanced with safeguarding.

Principles as set out in HM Inspectorate of Probation's Transitional Safeguarding practitioner briefing (2025), drawing on Research in Practice and the DHSC/DfE "Bridging the gap" briefing (2021).

Transition pathway: 17 to 25

This is a suggested model for discussion between Children's Services and ASC.

  1. 17

    Identify (from age 17)

    Children's Services flag care-experienced, CP and CIN young people with ongoing safeguarding risks (exploitation, missing episodes, mental health, substance use, offending, housing). Include young people known to the MET Panel, the knife carrying group and BCP’s under-18 focused deterrence workers, whose support ends at 18. Share the list with the ASC safeguarding team through an agreed transitions route.

  2. 17½

    Transition assessment and final child in care review

    Where the young person is likely to have care and support needs after 18 and an assessment would be of "significant benefit", start the transition assessment (in BCP, care-experienced young people can get this through the Preparing for Adulthood team). Agree a named ASC contact. NICE NG43 recommends a named worker the young person trusts, in place at least 6 months before and after transfer. Check the young person's consent and their views on who they trust.

    Final child in care review (before 18): where the young person is looked after, good practice is to discuss and use this toolkit at their final child in care review, chaired by the Independent Reviewing Officer (IRO). Invite the named ASC worker where appropriate, share the needs and risk summary and agree the handover plan. The IRO should receive the final pathway plan 20 working days before the 18th birthday and discuss it with the young person. If either is not satisfied, an extra review should be considered (IRO Handbook, paras 5.9–5.10).

  3. 18

    Joint handover meeting

    Social worker or PA, ASC worker, the young person (and a trusted adult or advocate) plus key agencies (health, housing, YJS or probation). Use the handover checklist. Children's Services stay involved until adult arrangements are in place.

  4. 18–21

    Relationship-based support

    Regular, flexible contact. Review risk at least quarterly and at trigger points: moving home, arrest, hospital attendance, a new relationship, a missed benefits claim or losing a job or course. Consider s42 enquiries for exploitation or self-neglect. Link to mentoring, EET and therapy (see Evidence-based approaches).

  5. 21+

    Don't drop off at 21

    Ofsted found support for BCP care leavers becomes less proactive after 21. Care leavers can keep their PA to 25 on request, and Staying Close support is becoming statutory up to 25. Make sure the young person knows how to ask.

  6. !

    Escalation

    Where risk increases, raise a s42 safeguarding concern, refer to MARAC (domestic abuse), make an NRM referral or Duty to Notify (exploitation), or refer to the high-risk panel. Escalation should add to relationship-based support, not replace it.

Care leaver categories: who is entitled to what

A young person’s care leaver category depends on when, and for how long, they were looked after. It decides the support they can get. Check it on the children’s record or with the Pathways Team.

CategoryWhoMain entitlements
EligibleAged 16 or 17 and still looked after, for at least 13 weeks in total since age 14, including some time after their 16th birthday.Still in care, with an IRO. Personal Adviser, needs assessment and a pathway plan (which includes the care plan).
RelevantAged 16 or 17, met the same 13-week rule, but has left care.Personal Adviser, pathway plan, and accommodation and financial support from the council.
Former relevantAged 18 to 25 and was an eligible or relevant child at 16 or 17. Most care leavers ASC meets are in this group.Personal Adviser to 21, or to 25 on request; pathway plan reviewed at least every 6 months; help with education and training; the BCP Local Offer.
QualifyingAged 16 to 20 and looked after at some point after their 16th birthday but does not meet the 13-week rule, or left care on a special guardianship order.Advice and assistance from the council, which can include help with education or training up to 25. No automatic Personal Adviser or pathway plan. Check which parts of the Local Offer apply.

Evidence-based approaches to deterrence and prevention

The Youth Endowment Fund (YEF) evidence is mostly about children and young people under 18. Use the YEF Toolkit with professional judgement, informed by evidence that maturity and desistance continue into the mid-20s.

ApproachYEF ratingHeadline estimateWhat it means for your practice

Avoid: prison-awareness ("scared straight") programmes and boot camps. The YEF rates both as harmful: they increase offending.

What a good offer looks like for this cohort: a trusted adult (mentoring for 6 months or more, weekly), trauma-specific therapy where needed, a route into work or training (the Youth Guarantee), and, where violence is present, a partnership deterrence approach in which police set clear consequences and ASC and partners provide a real support offer.

Practice guide

Relationship-based, trauma-informed practice with young adults who may have had many workers and good reasons not to trust services.

Do

  • Meet where and when suits them (cafés, walks, evenings, text first), within your lone-working and safety policies.
  • Keep the same worker wherever you can. Explain who you are and why you are there.
  • Ask them what has helped before and who they trust.
  • Treat non-engagement as a sign of need. Try again, and try differently.
  • Think about capacity at the moment of the decision, and about coercion. Consider executive functioning as well as understanding.
  • Read the children's file chronology. Trauma history can help explain current behaviour.
  • Celebrate small wins: registering with a GP, keeping a tenancy, attending an interview.

Avoid

  • Closing the case for "failure to engage" without a safeguarding review.
  • Treating an exploited young adult as making a "lifestyle choice".
  • Assuming that turning 18 means they are now able to protect themselves.
  • Making them retell their story. Share information with consent where possible, or where there is another lawful basis such as safeguarding.
  • Threat-based "scared straight" messages. The evidence shows they cause harm.
  • Adultification: seeing Black and minoritised young people as older and less vulnerable than they are.

Learning from Safeguarding Adults Reviews

A 2025 review of 19 SARs about young people in transition found the same problems again and again. Check your practice against them:

  • Support ended at 18 because of age, not because the need had gone.
  • Mental health referrals were not made because they were assumed not to meet adult thresholds.
  • Unsuitable housing increased the risk of exploitation.
  • Autism assessments were delayed and reasonable adjustments were missing.
  • "Did Not Attend" rules led to case closure instead of outreach.

Source: Teeswide Safeguarding Adults Board (2025). See Further guidance.

Conversation prompts

Getting to know them

  • What's going well for you at the moment?
  • Who's in your corner, and who would you call at 2am?
  • What did workers do in the past that helped, or didn't?

Safety

  • Is anyone asking you to do things you don't want to do?
  • Do you owe anyone money or favours?
  • Where do you feel safe? Where don't you?

Home and money

  • Where did you sleep last night? Is that OK for the next month?
  • Are your benefits sorted? Do you have ID and a bank account?

Future

  • What would a good year look like?
  • What's one thing we could sort out together this month?
